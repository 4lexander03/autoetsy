# Phase 1 — The Volume Play, Measured

**Date:** 2026-08-19 · Adds **W₂₀₀** (listings ≤6mo earning ≥$200/mo) alongside W₆ (≥$1,000),
plus the denominator: how many listings were created in that window at all.

---

## Why the new metric was needed

W₆ counts listings earning **≥$1,000/month**. It selects for *breakout single listings* and
is structurally blind to a shop earning $5,000/month from 200 listings at $25 each — which
is exactly the model a generator enables. Every "closed" verdict in the prior documents
means *no breakout listings*, not *no money here*.

## Finding 1 — the "hyper-specific tracker" thesis is dead

The niches recommended by every SEO blog — migraine trackers, pet health logs, home
maintenance checklists, plant care trackers — return **zero** listings under six months old
earning even $200/month. Not "low competition." **No demonstrated revenue at all.**

Those articles are recommending phantom markets. This is why the blogs' numbers
("competition 3/10, $1–4K/month potential") were treated as noise, and it was correct to
do so. Worth remembering as a general rule: content-marketing niche lists are not evidence.

## Finding 2 — the base rate, which nothing so far had established

**Roughly 1 in 250–400 new listings reaches $200/month.** Machine embroidery: 841 winners
out of 254,187 new listings = **0.33%**.

This is the sobering number the whole analysis was missing, and it disciplines the volume
strategy hard:

> **Volume multiplies quality; it does not substitute for it.**

- A generator producing 500 *mediocre* listings yields ~1–2 earners. Worthless.
- A generator producing 200 *genuinely good* listings, if we are meaningfully better than
  the median entrant, might yield 10–20 earners at $200+ → **$2,000–4,000/month**.

The plan cannot be "flood the category." It must be "produce a quality edge, then replicate
it across a taxonomy." Listing fees make brute force actively bad economics anyway:
6,000 listings ≈ $1,200 per four-month cycle, and Etsy flags that pattern.

## Finding 3 — the distribution ratio is itself a signal

W₂₀₀/W₆ measures whether a category's money is spread or concentrated:

- **Machine embroidery 27×, cross stitch 20×** → highly distributed. Hundreds of modest
  earners. A volume-plus-quality play fits.
- **Murder mystery 5.4×** → concentrated in a few breakouts. Confirms the earlier
  winner-take-most finding by a second, independent route.

Note murder mystery has the *best* success rate (0.77%, ~1 in 130) but the smallest pool
(5,606 new listings). Small, efficient, and already crowded with clones.

## Where this leaves the ranking

**Machine embroidery is now much stronger than it looked.** At the $1,000 threshold it was a
"narrow opening" (W₆ = 31). At $200 it is **841 successful new listings in six months** — the
largest pool of thriving new entrants of anything measured, with a 27× distribution ratio
that suits exactly the generate-and-replicate model.

**Cross stitch holds second** (245, 20×), and remains the only candidate needing no hardware.

**Mockup bundles enter third** (166, 0.40% — the best success rate of the three large
categories). Programmatically renderable, sells to other Etsy/POD sellers, and an earlier
observation is consistent: EmeraldMockupStudio, $3.00 × 498 sales = $1,494/mo at 2 months
old, 2900% growth. Weakness: $3–15 price points sit at or below the German fee floor
(GAMEPLAN §8.1), so it only works as high-volume bundles.

## Still unverified

- Whether the 841 embroidery winners are fonts/monograms specifically, or pictorial designs —
  this matters, because the font sub-segment is the generatable one.
- Denominators for the smaller candidates.
